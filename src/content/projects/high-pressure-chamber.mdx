## Overview

How fast does water evaporate at **500 bar**? The question matters for
advanced (notably sustainable) cooling systems — and it cannot be answered
from theory, because the standard tools assume ideal gases. For the Process
Engineering & Thermodynamics course (WB2543), our six-person team designed an
apparatus to measure the evaporation/condensation rate of water at 200 and
500 bar at room temperature: a **titanium pressure vessel**, its sealing and
safety systems, the instrumentation, and the measurement method itself.

## Where the textbook stops

The natural starting point, the **Hertz–Knudsen equation**, is derived from
ideal-gas assumptions — and above 500 bar the compressibility factor of water
vapour exceeds **1.5**, so those assumptions are simply false. The design
therefore uses the **Van der Waals equation** to relate pressure, volume and
amount of gas, and replaces a single theoretical prediction with an
**empirical strategy**: measure the rate over several small pressure
intervals bracketing the target (450–550, 490–510, …), filter outliers beyond
one standard deviation, and average toward the local rate.

That strategy dictated the measurement method. Of two candidates — continuous
**water-level tracking** versus a before/after **humidity measurement** — the
level method wins outright: it produces a continuous signal (effectively
unlimited data points) where the humidity method yields exactly one, far too
little for statistical filtering. Volume change follows directly from the
cylinder geometry: V = πr²·ΔH.

## Designing the vessel

A cylinder (~35 L, 50 cm inner height) beats a sphere: nearly as
good under pressure, far easier to manufacture — and its **constant
evaporation surface** is exactly what the level-based method needs.
**Titanium** was selected over aluminium and stainless steel for its
stiffness (deformation corrupts the height-to-volume conversion), corrosion
resistance, and — decisively — because it is **non-magnetic**, letting a
magnetic level gauge read the float _through the wall_ with no penetration.

Wall thickness follows EN 13445-3 with a design pressure of **650 bar**
(margin above the 500 bar operating point), the 300 mm bore and a conservative
weld-seam efficiency of 0.8:

e = P·Dᵢ / (2·f·z − P) = **15.4 mm** of titanium.

At 650 bar the pressure on the cap amounts to roughly **2,042 kN** — the
weight of a small building pulling the lid off. Divided by the 135 kN
capacity of an M24 bolt, the joint needs **16 bolts**, seated on a machined
rim that also carries the O-ring groove. The O-rings get **backup rings** on
both sides: at these pressures an unsupported elastomer simply extrudes into
the clearance gap.

## Safety as a design input

The vessel falls squarely into **Category IV** of the EU Pressure Equipment
Directive (2014/68/EU) — the heaviest conformity class. Beyond the
documentation burden, safety shaped the hardware: the tank is deliberately
over-engineered, and a **defined weak point** is built in so that if it ever
fails, it ruptures directionally, away from anyone near it — a controlled
release instead of a fragmenting vessel.

Instrumentation completes the design: a **Bourdon-tube gauge** for pressure
(capsule gauges stop far below 500 bar), a **bimetal thermometer** to confirm
the 300 K condition, the **magnetic level gauge** for the continuous height
signal, and a **3-way solenoid valve** (10–100 ms response) so the chamber can
be depressurised without disconnecting the compressor.

## Honest limits

The design is a paper machine: it was never built, and the report says so.
Even titanium deforms slightly at 650 bar, biasing the height-to-volume
conversion; small pressure intervals make height changes small relative to
measurement error (hence the averaging strategy); and cyclic-loading fatigue
was left out of scope entirely.

## Lessons learned

- **Check your equations' assumptions before your arithmetic.** The entire
  measurement strategy exists because Hertz–Knudsen silently assumes an ideal
  gas.
- **Choose instruments that don't fight the vessel.** The magnetic gauge
  through a non-magnetic wall removed every penetration a level sensor would
  otherwise need.
- **Regulation is load-bearing.** PED Category IV set the margins, the weld
  efficiency and the documentation — as rigidly as the 650 bar itself.
- **A good measurement method is statistics first.** Continuous data beat a
  single before/after point long before accuracy of any individual sensor
  mattered.
