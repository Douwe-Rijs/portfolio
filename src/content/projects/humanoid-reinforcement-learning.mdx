## Overview

This is the story of how I built my reinforcement-learning practice in two
stages. I started with the coursework: reasoning about value functions and
partial observability, implementing deep Q-learning and policy gradients, and
testing exploration methods in classic control environments. That work gave me
the algorithmic vocabulary and the experiment habits to move beyond toy tasks.

I then applied those lessons to a humanoid balance project. The second stage
added the engineering difficulty that textbook environments hide: many coupled
continuous actions, physical failure modes, long training campaigns and the
need to evaluate what the body actually does rather than trust a scalar return.
This made it way more difficult to solve with simple continious time policies than i had
originally thought.

The result is deliberately not framed as a solved walking controller. The
current SAC campaign reached 131.6 million transitions. But still as seen in the videos the result degraded hard,
not converging on a working policy.

## Part 1 — Coursework: building the RL foundations

The coursework broadened the project across different kinds of RL difficulty:

- **Delayed reward and memory:** deriving value comparisons and designing a
  memory-based policy for partially observed state, where the agent must retain
  a cue and a step count rather than react to the current observation alone.
- **CartPole:** implementing REINFORCE, then adding a learned value baseline,
  bootstrapped TD targets, actor-critic updates and off-policy actor-critic
  learning. PPO clipping limited the size of each policy update.
- **MountainCar:** exposing the exploration problem in an environment where
  random behaviour rarely reaches the useful reward. Rescaled states and
  intrinsic reward made exploration an explicit experimental variable.
- **Acrobot:** comparing Double DQN with and without intrinsic exploration,
  then implementing count-based uncertainty and Random Network Distillation
  as two ways to reward novel states.
- **Engineering layer:** building replay buffers, episode runners, return
  calculation, epsilon decay, training plots and checkpoint-oriented logging
  so algorithm changes could be compared rather than guessed at.

## Part 2 — Humanoid project: applying the foundations

### The problem

Humanoid control is unforgiving. The agent must coordinate many continuous joint
actions while keeping its centre of mass and torso orientation inside a narrow
balance envelope. A reward curve can improve while the robot still falls almost
immediately, especially when the reward, termination condition and evaluation
metric measure different things.

The project therefore treated evaluation as part of the controller design. A
run was not considered successful because it had trained for longer or produced
an attractive frame. It had to survive the fixed balance suite without falling,
excessive tilt or unacceptable drift.

### The control stack

The humanoid environment exposes a structured observation, the SAC policy emits
continuous joint actions, and the reward combines task progress with posture and
stability terms. The evaluator reports physical failure modes separately from
training statistics.

The important change in scale is from discrete textbook actions to a body with
many coupled joints. Techniques that look separate in CartPole or Acrobot become
design constraints here: replay quality, exploration, reward shaping,
termination and evaluation all affect whether a policy can keep its balance.

### What failed, and why that matters

The final diagnostic report recorded **0/5** passing episodes, a mean duration
of **0.39 seconds**, and a mean maximum tilt of **39.6 degrees**. Every episode
was classified as both `fell:too_low` and `tilt_exceeded_too_often`. The policy
did not merely miss a target; it lost the physical state the task required it
to preserve.

I also kept a behaviour-cloning warm-start run as a comparison rather than
silently merging it with the SAC results. Its later checkpoints also failed the
five-episode gate, which suggests that initialisation alone was not enough to
solve the control problem.

## Lessons learned

- **Evaluate the body, not only the loss.** Duration, tilt and drift explain a
  controller's failure more directly than a training curve alone.
- **Reward shaping is a systems decision.** A reward that encourages motion but
  does not protect the balance envelope can produce apparently active yet
  unusable behaviour.
- **Checkpoint videos are part of the experiment record.** They make regressions
  and failure modes inspectable without rerunning a long campaign.
- **Negative results can be engineering results.** The current policy is not a
  solved humanoid controller, but the run infrastructure makes the next
  intervention testable: reward calibration, termination timing and controller
  assistance can be changed one variable at a time.
