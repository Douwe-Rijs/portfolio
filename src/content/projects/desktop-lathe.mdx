## Overview

A miniature but genuinely working **metal lathe**: a DC spindle motor in a
bolted sheet-metal headstock, an ER11 collet holding the workpiece, and a
stepper-driven cross-slide feeding the cutting tool. The machine was designed
in SolidWorks, sized with hand calculations, sanity-checked with FEM — and then
actually machined, assembled and run.

## Sizing the machine by hand

Every component traces back to one number: the **cutting force on the tool**.
With a specific cutting force of 800 MPa, a feed of 0.05 mm/rev and a cut depth
of 0.25 mm, the force comes out at just **10 N** — small, but it propagates
through the whole design:

- **Motor power:** 10 N at a cutting speed of 0.5 m/s demands **5 W**. The DC
  motor delivers up to **22.32 W** at 20 V (from its stall torque and no-load
  speed) — comfortable margin, verified rather than assumed.
- **Positioning error:** the steppers carry a worst-case offset of 5% of one
  step — **0.09° per step**, negligible against the mechanics.
- **Tool-holder stiffness:** measured in both X and Y; deflection stayed at or
  below roughly 0.1 mm.

<Split src={femDisplacement} alt="SolidWorks FEM displacement plot of the ER11 chuck collet under cutting load, showing a rainbow gradient with maximum deflection in the micrometre range at the tip." caption="The FEM verdict on the collet holder: micrometre-scale deflection under cutting load — no extra bearing needed." reverse>

The most consequential check was on the **collet holder**: would the motor
shaft bend under cutting load without a support bearing? A static FEM study
(cutting force applied as the hand-calculated force + moment) showed
deflections in the micrometre range and no concerning stress concentrations —
so the extra bearing was **deliberately left out**. One simulation deleted an
entire subassembly.

The headstock packs the DC spindle motor and stepper into a bolted frame of
steel angle profiles — every joint mechanical, no welds, so the enclosure
could be corrected and re-machined as the build progressed. The cross-slide
runs on ground rods with a leadscrew per axis.

## It runs

The proof is watching it cut. Feed the tool in, and the machine behaves the
way the numbers said it would — with one honest caveat from the error budget:
it is **not quiet**, and gets louder as the tool approaches the collet, simply
because more material (and its burrs) is being pushed aside per revolution.

## Lessons learned

- **Trace every part to a load case.** The 10 N cutting force sized the motor,
  justified omitting a bearing, and set the stiffness target — one number,
  five decisions.
- **Simulate to delete, not to decorate.** The FEM run existed to answer a
  yes/no question (bearing or not), and it removed real parts and cost.
- **Margins you calculate are margins you trust.** Needing 5 W and having
  22 W was a verified fact, not a hope.
- **The desk is the final exam.** Stiffness, noise and burrs never show up in
  CAD — building the machine is what makes the design honest.
